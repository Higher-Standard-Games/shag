## 0. A late uplink: resync storm and dropped backlog

### What his net graph says

The ping on the net graph is measured by the server: the time from sending a
snapshot to receiving its ACK (`GameServer.recordAckRoundTrip`, smoothed by
0.25). Snapshots reached him at the full 62/s with no gaps, so his downlink was
healthy. The ~850 ms was on the way *up*: commands and ACKs queued on his
side, the shape of bufferbloat behind a saturated upload. (He was uploading a
video at the time; any upload, a cloud sync or a weak Wi-Fi uplink does the
same.) We cannot remove the delay, but the game must survive it without
teleporting.

Our own upstream is modest but wasteful: a command message is 42 bytes, each
command is sent three times (the redundancy is useless over TCP), plus a 5-byte
ACK per snapshot, about 440 messages and 20 KB a second.

### What the code does with it

1. **Client: resynchronisation.** `V2NetworkClient.applySnapshot` replays at
   most `MAX_REPLAY = 32` commands. At 850 ms round trip about 105 are
   unacknowledged, so every snapshot takes the resync branch: adopt the
   server's position — a round trip old — and discard the unacknowledged
   commands. The server still runs those commands when they arrive, so the
   following snapshots move the player forward again. Back, forward, back, for
   the whole spike.
2. **Server: backlog dropped.** When a stall ends, the burst of commands is
   trimmed to `MAX_INPUT_BUFFER = 12` by `dropOldest`, which does not count the
   dropped commands as simulated. Their movement is lost on the server while
   the client predicted it: another correction, and a large one after a long
   stall.

### Measurement

`.cache/netsim/e2e-spike.ts` runs the real `V2NetworkClient` against the real
`FPSMovementSubsystem` (flat floor) over a simulated in-order link: 80 ms base
round trip, 10 ms jitter, 60 FPS client, 30 s per run. The player runs, turns,
changes strafe every 400 ms and hops. "Spike" adds delay both ways for a
window; "bloat" ramps the uplink only to ~900 ms over 2 s, holds 3 s, drains,
twice.

| Variant | 800 ms spike ×3: corrections > 5 u / largest | 400 ms spike ×3 | Uplink bloat: corrections > 5 u / largest / visible jumps | Resyncs (spike, bloat) | Queue avg (ticks) | Seconds with > 96 ms extra queue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shipped (replay 32, buffer 12) | 72 / 160 u | 44 / 89 u | 300 / 92 u / 106 | 12, 48 | 4–5 | 0 |
| Replay unlimited | 18 / 99 u | 8 / 28 u | 34 / 10 u / 0 | 0, 0 | 4–5 | 0 |
| Replay unlimited, buffer 200 | 6 / 9 u | 4 / 7 u | 1 / 5 u / 0 | 0, 0 | 31–49 | **16–22 of 30** |
| Replay unlimited, buffer 64, catch-up above 6 queued | 16 / 9 u | 5 / 9 u | **0 / 5 u / 0** | 0, 0 | 2–3 | 0–0.8 |

Steady 80 ms with 10 ms jitter: 0 corrections over 5 u in every variant, which
matches "choppiness improved". A 300 ms client freeze at 40 FPS: 9 corrections
(max 9 u) shipped; catch-up makes it 10 (max 27–32 u), so catch-up needs
tuning around claimed guesses.

A bigger buffer alone keeps every real command but plays them late: the
backlog drains at one command a second, so after a spike the player carries
up to half a second of extra input delay for a minute. **Catch-up** fixes that:
while more than a few commands are queued, the player's movement runs two
steps in a tick until the backlog is gone (a 60-command backlog clears in
half a second). That is the Quake behaviour — run the client's commands, never
guess past a short grace — bounded to two steps a tick.

Reconciliation CPU (flat world, V8): 3.7 ms per second of play today; with an
unlimited replay ~15 ms/s averaged over the bloat run (~40 ms/s during the
bloat itself). Real maps and Firefox cost several times more, so the budget
should be high (about one second of commands, 125) rather than unlimited,
together with the confirmation shortcut below.

### Fix

Client (no protocol change):

- Raise `MAX_REPLAY` to about one second of commands, and keep the resync only
  as the last resort beyond it.
- **Skip the replay when the server confirms the prediction.** Store the
  predicted position and velocity per command (with the hidden state of §1);
  when the snapshot matches the stored state at `ackedInputSeq` within a small
  epsilon, the current prediction is already right and nothing needs
  replaying. With §1 fixed, that is nearly every snapshot, so the replay cost
  drops in normal play too (useful in Firefox).

Server:

- `MAX_INPUT_BUFFER` 12 → about 64, still well under the flood guard of 64
  arrivals per tick.
- Catch-up: while the queue is deeper than ~6, run the player's movement twice
  in the tick. The prototype double-ticked the whole one-player world; the real
  change runs a second movement step for that entity only. Per-tick world
  effects are what made "two steps in a tick" a rejected option in v0.9.35, so
  this needs:
  - the effect clock from a per-entity step counter instead of `tickNum` (the
    client already counts commands, and only rates are shared),
  - knockback drag advanced per movement step (the client already does),
  - lifts: the client steps lifts per command; the server steps them per world
    tick. A catch-up step on a lift is a known small mismatch.
- Keep the 2-tick unclaimed grace; re-evaluate claimed guesses once catch-up
  exists, since catch-up makes guessing far less necessary.

Upstream traffic (protocol change, lower priority): one message per frame
carrying that frame's new commands and the ACK, no redundancy over TCP — about
60–125 messages a second instead of ~440. Smaller queues on a congested uplink.

## 1. Reconciliation replays from the wrong movement state

### What happens

On every snapshot `V2NetworkClient.applySnapshot` resets position, velocity,
grounded and knockback age to the server's values at the acknowledged command,
then replays the unacknowledged commands. Everything else the movement profile
carries is **not** rewound: slide (`isSliding`, start, cooldown and exit
anchors), crouch view anchors, ledge grab, ladder `climbing`, `proneInWater`,
water-jump and ladder-detach deadlines, `jumpConsumed`, `smoothedY`.
`resetPhysicsState` additionally resets the wall jump to nothing. The replay
therefore starts at an old position with the *newest* hidden state.

The slide shows it most clearly. Press crouch at command S while running fast;
the next snapshot acknowledges A < S:

1. The replay starts at A with `isSliding = true` (the state after S).
2. Command A+1 has no crouch, so the slide "ends" — and `Slide.exit` starts the
   1.5 s slide cooldown at A+1.
3. At S the slide cannot start: it is on cooldown. The replay loses the 1.4×
   entry boost and the slide's low friction.
4. The client is moved back. The next snapshot (now past S) carries the boosted
   velocity, but the client's slide state was lost in step 3, so its replay
   applies ground friction and falls behind again, until the slide ends.

The wall jump handles this already with `WallJump.beginStep`, which drops
cooldowns predicted at or after the replayed step. The slide has no
equivalent. `ReconcileParity.test.ts` passes because its slide starts at the
acknowledged command, never after it.

### Measurement

`.cache/netsim/parity-probe.ts` (scratch, gitignored) feeds the real
`V2NetworkClient` snapshots that are **exactly** the authority's state, every
2 ticks (the live 62.5 Hz cadence), acknowledged `lag` ticks back. Any
correction is replay disagreement, not the network. 125 Hz, so lag 12 ≈ 96 ms.

| Scenario | Lag 2 | Lag 6 | Lag 12 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slide starting inside the window | 52/79 corrections > 0.5 u, max 5.6 u | 52/77, max 12.8 u | 52/74, max **36.4 u** |
| Slide, release, crouch again | 36/99, max 5.6 u | 36/97, max 12.8 u | 36/94, max 36.4 u |
| Slide, jump-cancel | 25/99, max 5.6 u | 25/97, max 12.8 u | 25/94, max 36.4 u |
| Run, tap jump, hold jump (autohop), hold jump while turning, slide starting at the ack | 0 | 0 | 0 |

Every slide, by every player, at every ping. Low-ping players see a few-unit
nudge; at 80 ms it is the "teleport back", then forward again as later
snapshots arrive.

### Fix (prototyped)

Keep the client's own hidden movement state per command, and restore the one
at the acknowledged command before replaying; position, velocity, grounded and
knockback still come from the server. The prototype captures every
non-tuning field of the profile after each predicted command and restores it
before `applySnapshot`. Result: **0 corrections in every scenario above**,
including the slide cases.

For the real change:

- Give `MovementProfile` (and `Slide`, `LedgeGrab`, `WallJump`) an explicit
  `captureState()` / `restoreState()` pair rather than reflection, so a new
  field cannot be missed silently. A test should compare a captured state
  against the profile's own field list.
- Store the captured state in `InputHistory` beside each command (about 40
  numbers × 125/s; pruned with the command).
- In `applySnapshot`, restore the state at `ackedInputSeq`, then apply the
  server's grounded flag; `resetPhysicsState`'s partial resets go away.
- Add the slide-inside-the-window scenarios to `ReconcileParity.test.ts`.
- Then re-measure the wall-jump and ledge-grab windows with restore and without
  the suppression in `applySnapshot`. The suppression (`grabReconcileSkipCount`,
  `wallJumpReconcileSkipCount`, `MAX_SUPPRESSION_MS`) exists because replay
  disagreed there; with the right starting state it may no longer be needed,
  which also removes a source of delayed, larger corrections.

Client-only: no protocol, demo or server change. Risk is in the movement
prediction path, so it needs `test:simulation`, `test:physics`, the parity
tests and a live check.

## 2. Aim stutter seen by others (demo ADS)

### What a demo shows

A demo is recorded on the server. Each frame (every 2 ticks) stores the server
state — including each player's applied aim — and, per player, how many
commands **arrived** during the frame (`GameServer.handleInput` records on
arrival). So a demo measures both the uplink's burstiness and what other
players saw.

The server applies at most one queued command per tick. When the queue is
empty it repeats the last command: the aim holds. After two unclaimed repeats
further guesses are claimed, and when the stalled commands arrive they are
dropped as late, so the aim then jumps to the newest one. A silence of four or
more ticks therefore shows as hold-then-jump. On a zoomed ADS view of that
player, in a demo or to an opponent, it reads as a stuttering gun. The
player's own view is not affected: it is predicted locally.

### Local demos

`.cache/netsim/demo-aim.ts <file.shagdemo>` reports, per player, commands
arriving per frame, silent runs, and how often the applied aim holds while
the player is turning.

| Player (local demos) | Ping | Silent runs ≥ 4 ticks | Aim held while turning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Player_3771, Player_9568, Player_3300, Player_1823 | 7–13 | rare | 0.3–0.7 % |
| Player_7699 (long match) | 10 | 36 in 50k frames | 1.2 % |
| Player_1327, Player_8372, Player_4337, Player_8846 | 17–24 | up to 138 in 11k frames | 3.5–6.9 % |
| Player_7699 (`washoi_snd1_snd_20261018_230539`) | **73** | 46 in 2.9k frames (~1/s) | **18.6 %** |

(Bots show ~15–24 % because they send fewer commands; ignore them.) At 73 ms,
45 % of 2-tick frames brought no command and others brought 3–8: the commands
arrive in clumps. The sample is short (113 turning frames). The production
demo will give anti-pizza's own numbers: drop it in `public/data/demos/` and
run the script.

### Candidates

| Option | Effect | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A. Adaptive per-player input buffer: hold the queue at a depth sized from that player's measured arrival jitter (for example p95 silent run) | Repeats and claims become rare; smooth aim and motion for others | That player's commands apply later by the buffer depth (their hits are still judged at their `viewTick`); more replay on their client |
| B. Smooth remote and demo view angles on the rendering side | Hides holds and jumps for viewers | Adds display latency to every remote aim; hides rather than fixes |
| C. Bundle one command message per frame (TCP makes the 3× redundancy useless) | Fewer messages; does not remove clumping | Protocol change |

A is the real fix and was the open "WO-10 jitter buffer" item in
`Telemetry.ts`. `Telemetry` already computes per-client RFC 3550 jitter from
arrival times, which is the input A needs. Measure with the production demo
before choosing the target depth.

## 3. Slow mouse movement on Linux Firefox

Our path is lossless. `CameraInputHandler` adds `movementX/Y × sensitivity`
per `mousemove` event straight into the view angles, with no rounding, dead
zone or filter. The only other writes to the view angles are teleporter or
spawn yaw and the post-game freeze. Nothing from the network writes the local
view.

The symptom matches Firefox's own pointer-lock bugs on Linux:

- [Bug 2012124](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2012124) (Firefox
  146, Ubuntu, Wayland): `movementY` stays zero on slow movement under pointer
  lock. The developer reproduced it only in out-of-process iframes. Closed as a
  duplicate of
- [Bug 2036028](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=2036028), "[Wayland]
  Pointer lock doesn't work well on OOP iframe", fixed in **Firefox 152** by
  moving Wayland pointer lock onto the common flow.
- [Bug 1930525](https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/show_bug.cgi?id=1930525) (Fedora,
  Wayland): wrong pointer-lock speed, mouse-dependent; also closed against
  2036028.
- An older, general report: movement values were affected by
  `devicePixelRatio`, so slow movement could round to zero
  ([W3C bug 28029](https://www.w3.org/Bugs/Public/show_bug.cgi?id=28029)).

**Confirmed browser-side:** Chromium on the same Linux machine registers slow
movement correctly. Still unknown: his Firefox version (the Wayland rework
shipped in 152), Wayland or X11, display scale and page zoom.

What we can do:

- Tell Linux players that Chromium is the reliable browser for now (the
  release notes and the welcome guide are the places).
- A small console command (`m_diag`, not developer-gated) that records five
  seconds of raw `mousemove` events and prints event count, per-axis sum, the
  share of events with a zero axis, the distinct values seen,
  `devicePixelRatio` and the user agent. A player runs it while moving slowly
  diagonally and pastes the line. It turns "feels like" into a number, and
  becomes a reproducible report for Mozilla.
- Workarounds to try meanwhile: Firefox under X11 (`MOZ_ENABLE_WAYLAND=0`) and
  page zoom at 100 %.

## 4. Measurement gaps

- **Corrections are invisible to players.** `NetGraph` has a
  "corrections/sec" row, but `recordReconciliation()` is never called. Calling
  it when a reconcile moves the visual position by more than ~0.5 u (and
  showing the largest correction) lets a tester's screenshot say what happened.
- **Upload delay looks like ping.** The client can measure its own input
  delay: it knows when it sent the command a snapshot acknowledges. Showing
  "input delay" next to ping on the net graph would have told him at a glance
  that his upload was queueing.
- **Server input policy outcomes are not exported.** Starved ticks, claimed
  guesses, late drops, drains and buffer overflows per player would make the
  next round a number. They could ride in the match telemetry log, or in demo
  frames (a demo-format change).

## Recommended order

1. Client reconciliation (§0 client part, §1): restore hidden movement state at
   the ack, skip the replay when the ack confirms the prediction, raise the
   replay budget to one second. Client-only, no protocol change, and it covers
   both the slide corrections and the resync storm.
2. Net graph: wire the correction counter and add input delay (§4). A few
   lines; makes every later field report a measurement.
3. Server: buffer 64 plus catch-up steps (§0 server part). Networking-critical;
   needs the per-entity step clock and the full regression set.
4. Analyse the production demo; decide whether an adaptive input buffer is
   still needed once catch-up exists (§2).
5. Mouse: recommend Chromium on Linux; `m_diag` (§3).
6. One command message per frame without redundancy (§0, protocol change).
7. Export server input-policy counters (§4).

## What was implemented

The design changed in one respect from the plan in §0. Catch-up on top of the
v0.9.35 policy would have been a lag-switch exploit: a starved tick there
repeats the last command as a free step, so catching the stalled commands up
afterwards would add their movement on top. Catch-up is only sound if a starved
player takes no step, so the server now waits (Quake behaviour) and owes the
tick, bounded so nobody can hang frozen.

Client (`V2NetworkClient`, `InputHistory`, `MovementProfile`, `Slide`,
`WallJump`, `LedgeGrab`):

- Every predicted or replayed command stores the state it left: position,
  velocity, grounded, knockback age, the movement profile's full state
  (`saveState()`; explicit per field) and, on maps with movers, lift state.
- A snapshot that matches the stored state for the command it acknowledges
  (0.01 tolerance, the float32 wire precision) is **confirmed**: no replay, and
  the client keeps its own double-precision state.
- Otherwise the replay resumes from the server's position, velocity, grounded
  and age plus the stored movement state at the ack (`loadState()` and
  `setGrounded()`); without a stored state it falls back to the old reset.
- Replay budget: two seconds of commands (250). A resync clears the history.
- The knockback identity is `ackedInputSeq - age` (steps and commands advance
  together under the new server policy).
- Net graph: `corr` / `max` (corrections over 0.5 u) and `up` / `down` (each
  one-way timing above its 30-second minimum; the minimum cancels the clock
  offset, no clock sync needed).

Server (`FPSMovementSubsystem`, `MovementValidator`):

| Rule | Value |
| --- | --- |
| A tick runs the next command; none queued → the player waits and owes the tick | — |
| Catch-up: a second step in the tick while ticks are owed and the queue is this deep | 12 commands |
| Slow catch-up: queue at least this deep for a whole second, ticks owed | 3 (else the oldest is given up, as before) |
| Longest wait before guessing (claimed, as in v0.9.35) | 500 ms (62.5 ticks) |
| Guesses ahead of the newest real command | 32 |
| A player never heard from steps with a neutral command after the wait | — |
| Input buffer / flood guard / time budget | 250 / 250 per tick / 2 s |
| Effect clock and knockback drag age | per movement step (`tickNum + per-player offset`) |
| Weapons in a catch-up tick | newest command, plus action buttons and weapon request of the earlier one |

Bots (`BotClient`) send one command per server tick by the clock; on Windows
the 8 ms timer fires about every 15.6 ms and bots had sent half the tick rate,
leaning on the old repeat.

### Why a VPN hurts this game and not Warsow

Warsow sends UDP datagrams. Each stands alone: a lost or late one is
skipped, the next carries fresh state, and its commands repeat recent input,
so a loss costs a packet's worth of time.

This game runs over WebSocket, which is TCP. TCP delivers in order, so one
lost segment holds back everything behind it until it is retransmitted, and
the retransmission timer starts at about 200 ms and doubles on each repeat.
A VPN adds loss, jitter and often MTU trouble (fragmentation, black holes).
A VPN that tunnels over TCP itself (OpenVPN in TCP mode) is the worst case:
both layers retransmit, and the inner stream's timers fight the outer one's.
The same lost packet that costs Warsow 8 ms costs us a 200–400 ms stall,
which the net graph shows as "ping jumps to 200".

The server now absorbs a stall of up to 500 ms at no cost to the player: it
waits, then runs every command when they arrive. Past 500 ms it guesses
(`MAX_PAUSE_TICKS`), and a wrong guess is a correction. A link whose stalls
regularly exceed half a second — a retransmission that itself is lost, or a
TCP-over-TCP tunnel — still teleports occasionally. That matches "better,
but still happens", and "no issues" without the VPN.

### Options

| Option | Effect | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **WebTransport datagrams** (`WebTransportTransport.ts`, kept for this) | UDP-like delivery in the browser: no head-of-line blocking, a loss costs one packet. Commands would then need redundancy again (the 3× copies are useless only over TCP) | Server-side Node support is the blocker (see `AGENTS.md`); Chrome and Firefox support it client-side |
| Player advice | Avoid VPNs for play, or use a UDP-based one (WireGuard) rather than OpenVPN over TCP | Documentation only (player manual troubleshooting) |
| Raise the pause cap toward 1 s | Absorbs longer stalls without corrections | Anyone can hold themselves frozen twice as long; simulation showed little gain at 1 s (§ "Measured") |
| One command message per frame | Fewer, larger upstream messages; less to queue on a congested uplink | Protocol change; does not remove head-of-line blocking |

Recommendation: player advice now; WebTransport when Node can serve it; leave
the pause cap unless VPN players turn out to be common.

- His VPN's protocol and loss rate are unknown; the explanation above is the
  standard TCP behaviour, not a measurement of his tunnel.
- His production match recordings were not analysed here: copying them off
  the host was declined because they contain other players' data.

## Assumptions and unverified

- The parity probe and the end-to-end simulation use a flat floor (and a wall),
  not a real map; they isolate the netcode from collision detail. CPU figures
  are V8 on that floor.
- The end-to-end simulation is one player on a flat floor. Catch-up on a
  lift, knockback during a wait, and several laggy players at once are covered
  by reasoning and unit tests, not by a live match.
- No session with injected latency was run (there is no fake-lag option).
  The production test is the confirmation: fine at ~80 ms without a VPN,
  occasional teleports with one (see "Field result").
- Re-sim bundles recorded by an older server will not reproduce under the new
  input policy; bundles from the same build do.
- The bufferbloat diagnosis rests on one net graph screenshot (full snapshot
  rate, ~860 ms ACK round trip); his upload at the time is circumstantial.
- The prototype restores state by reflection over the profile's fields; the
  real fix needs an explicit capture.
- The production demo was not available locally, so anti-pizza's own arrival
  pattern is not measured; the 73 ms local sample is short.
- Which Firefox bug he hits (version, Wayland or X11) is unconfirmed; that it
  is Firefox is confirmed by Chromium on the same machine.
